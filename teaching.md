## Approach

My goal is for students to grow as independent thinkers. The main pillars of my teaching approach are:
- Lecture structure: Interwoven mix of learning new material and **active engagement** with the concepts through practice problems, derivations, and discussions of progressive difficulty (and, for some courses, small-group laboratory experiments). 
- **Community learning** spirit: Instructor and students as a team work to develop deep intuition for course material.
- **Continuous assessment**: An effective way to assess one's own learning and stay on top of material. Assessments are designed to make learners think, apply the concepts to a range of problems, and probe the validity of the systems of equations taught in class. 
- Constructive **feedback** is always welcome. 
- I make every attempt to get to **know each of my students**, often through one-on-one meetings at the start of the course.

### Geophysical Fluid Dynamics

The aim of this course is to develop a rigorous and firm understanding of the fluid dynamics that describes atmospheric and oceanic circulation. 
The course's main learning objectives are to 
(i) immerse ourselves in the language of vector calculus, tensors, and partial differential equations, with the goal that when we see a differential equation, it speaks to us; 
(ii) attain fluency with core analytical tools including scaling analysis and asymptotic expansions; 
(iii) derive the conservation laws and Navier Stokes equation from first principles; 
(iv) describe the (relative) role of each term in the rotating Navier Stokes equation; 
(v) apply the various approximations to the governing equations and describe when these are valid. 
Additional topics include tracer transport, thermodynamic equation, and waves. 
There will also be some laboratory sessions where experiments will be run in small groups, designed to deepen understanding of lecture material. 
This is a graduate course open to PhD students in all departments. 
Cross-listed between Atmospheric & Climate Science and School of Oceanography. 
Required for Dynamics PhD students. 
4 lectures/week.

*Recommended reference material:* Joe Pedlosky's textbook, Joe Pedlosky's lecture notes, Rick Salmon’s lecture notes, Geoff Vallis' textbook.

### Physics of Climate

The overarching goal of this course is to develop intuition for the physics of Earth’s climate. 
Its main learning objectives are to 
(i) design simple models for the physics of climate, write out their equations, and solve them; 
(ii) interpret solutions of various simple models to explain how Earth’s climate has changed, is changing, and will change; 
(iii) assess popular arguments made about Earth's climate using the physics learnt in this course and the principles of logic (a brief introduction to logic will be given). 
This is an undergraduate course that assumes a knowledge of calculus. 
Required for majors. 
3 lectures/week.

*Recommended reference material:* ‘Global Physical Climatology’ by Dennis Hartmann, one of the many online courses on logic, such as Stanford Online's 'Introduction to Logic' [https://www.coursera.org/learn/logic-introduction](https://www.coursera.org/learn/logic-introduction){:target="_blank"} or Ian Schnee's 'Logic Course Adventure' [https://ianschnee.com](https://ianschnee.com){:target="_blank"}

### Probabilistic and Scientific Machine Learning

In this course, we embark on a mathematical journey through the core concepts underlying probabilistic and scientific machine learning (ML). 
The course emphasises deriving the foundations and developing intuition for them. 
For probabilistic ML, the learning objectives are 
(i) derive the foundational probability rules; define the expectation, variance, and covariance; and prove key theorems involving expectation and variance of random variables; 
(ii) outline the main properties of various probability distributions, the types of systems they describe, and their limitations; 
(iii) explain core concepts in machine learning and statistical inference as well as their formulations, such as entropy, information content, likelihood, K-L divergence, loss functions; 
(iv) conduct maximum likelihood estimation, derive maximum a posteriori expressions, and maximise entropy; 
(v) describe simple neural networks: what they are, how they can be used, and why they work. 
For scientific ML, the objective is to explain how a selection of techniques work, including Physics-Informed Neural Networks, Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics, and (time permitting) Symbolic Regression.

I developed and taught this course at the African Institute for Mathematical Sciences in South Africa, as part of the *AI for Science* Master’s program, and will teach a version of this course at the UW.

*Recommended reference material:* ‘Deep Learning’ by Bishop & Bishop (Chapters 2, 3, 4), Steve Brunton’s YouTube channel.

## AI use policy for learning

Here, the UW's AI Governance Committee's working definition of AI is adopted: 
"AI refers to computational systems and models that operate with varying degrees of autonomy and produce outputs not fully specified by explicit rules, together with the practices through which people develop, deploy, and use them." 
(Source: [UW AIGC Memo D](https://www.ai.uw.edu/ai-governance-committee-summer-2026-update/){:target="_blank"})

AI presents many opportunities for learning, and I encourage my students to use *reliable* AI tools as a learning supplement, 
e.g., to clear any confusion, to get tailored practice problems on challenging material when preparing for exams. 
Assessments for my courses are in-person, so please make sure your preparation for them does not build reliance on these tools. 
Use of AI on any other submitted work must be declared truthfully. 
Finally, please remember that using AI does not replace independent thinking.
